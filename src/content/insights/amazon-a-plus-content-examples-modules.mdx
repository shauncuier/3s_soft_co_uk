Most articles about A+ Content examples show you a gallery of pretty pages and leave you to work out why they are pretty. This one does the opposite. We take one set of pages we built, for the Ivan Troy belt range on Amazon.com, and walk down them module by module, explaining what each module is for and the question it answers for a buyer. The full project is in our [Ivan Troy case study](/case-studies/ivan-troy-amazon-brand-story/).

A quick reminder of the two tools. **A+ Content** replaces the plain text description on a listing with designed modules. **Brand Story** is a separate carousel that appears on every listing a brand owns under the heading "From the brand". Both need Brand Registry. The sizes for every module are in our [A+ Content image sizes guide](/insights/amazon-a-plus-content-image-sizes-guidelines/).

## What does the Brand Story do that A+ Content doesn't?

A+ Content is per product. Brand Story is per brand. That one difference tells you what to put in it: anything that is true of everything you sell. For Ivan Troy that is three things.

**The brand banner** carries the one line the brand wants every buyer to read, "Designed in LA. Made in Italy.", over a photograph of leather being cut by hand. There is no product in it on purpose. The banner is the background module of the Brand Story and it is the first thing a buyer sees when they scroll past the bullet points.

**Three category cards** point to the belts, the men's shoes and the women's shoes, bags and belts. These are Brand ASIN & Store Showcase cards: a portrait image, a short line of text and a link into that part of the range. A buyer who landed on a belt and wonders whether the brand does shoes finds out without leaving the page.

**A brand Q&A** answers the four questions the brand gets asked most: where the products are made, how to choose a belt size, how Italian shoe sizes compare with US sizes, and how to care for leather. This is the Brand Q&A module, and it is the most underused module on Amazon. If your customer service inbox has the same three questions every week, this is where the answers go.

## What goes at the top of an A+ page?

The Ivo Black Croco page opens with **a hero with a model shot**: the colour name in large type, the leather and finish underneath, and the belt being worn. This is what the Standard Image Header with Text module is for. Its job is not to sell. Its job is to confirm, in one glance, that the buyer is on the right page and that the product is real and photographed properly. Put the single strongest photograph you own here.

Directly under it sit **four numbered feature callouts** on one product photograph: the crocodile-embossed pattern, the polished buckle, the 35 mm dress width that fits standard trouser loops, and made in Italy by hand. Numbered callouts answer "what am I actually getting?" in the order a buyer's eye travels. Keep the labels short. The number does the pointing; the text only names the feature.

## Which modules build trust in the middle of the page?

**A "Handmade in Italy" detail** comes next: a close-up of the embossed leather and a short note on how each belt is cut, pressed and edge-stitched. This is a single-image-and-text module, and its purpose is proof. The hero made a claim about quality; this module shows the stitching close enough to judge it.

**Two styling pairs**, "Sharp with a suit" and "Clean with denim", follow. Each is a photograph and one line of advice. A three- or four-image module suits this well. Styling modules do two jobs: they help a buyer picture the product in their own life, and they quietly widen the market, from "a belt for my suit" to "a belt I would wear at the weekend".

**A "One belt, six shades" range module** shows all six colours side by side with the same leather, buckle and fit. For a product that comes in variants this is the module that stops a buyer leaving to search for "the same belt in brown". Amazon's variation widget shows swatches; this module shows the actual product in each colour, which is what people want to see.

## What should the bottom of an A+ page do?

The last two modules on the Ivo page remove the two biggest reasons a belt gets returned or not bought at all.

**A "Find your size" chart** states the rule in one sentence, order a belt two inches larger than your trouser waist, and then lists trouser waists from 28 to 50 inches against belt sizes from 30 to 52. We build this as a text or specification module rather than an image so it is readable on a phone and can be updated without a designer. Wrong-size orders are a return, a refund and sometimes a one-star review. A size chart on the page is the cheapest fix there is.

**A comparison table across all six variants** closes the page. The Standard Comparison Chart module lets you put up to six products in columns with a thumbnail and a row for each attribute: leather, buckle, width, sizes, where it is made and what each colour pairs with. The current colour is highlighted and the other five link to their own listings. The table does what the range module started: it keeps a buyer inside the brand's range rather than back in Amazon search.

Every colourway uses the same seven modules in the same order. Only the photography and a few lines of colour-specific copy change. That is deliberate. A buyer who opens two colours should feel they are looking at one brand, and the brand should be able to add a seventh colour by swapping images rather than commissioning a new design. In A+ Content Manager you can duplicate a published page and apply it to a new ASIN, which is how we publish each variant.

At the time of writing the Ivo Black Croco listing is live with the Brand Story and A+ Content shown here, and the other five colourways are being published in turn. We do not report sales figures for this project, because we do not have them and we would not invent them.

## Which modules should you use for your own product?

Start from the questions buyers ask before they buy, not from the module list. For most products that gives you:

- **One hero** that confirms what the product is
- **One features module** that names what the buyer gets
- **One proof module**: a close-up, a material, a certification, a process
- **One use or context module**: the product in someone's life
- **One decision module**: a size guide, a comparison chart or a specification table

That is five modules, which is the Basic A+ Content limit. If your product has variants, swap the use module for a range module and let the comparison chart do the rest.
